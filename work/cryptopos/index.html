CryptoPos

Merchant crypto acceptance fails on economics, not technology. The QR code has worked for a decade; the business case still doesn’t. This prototype makes the economics visible at the counter — and configurable.

Premise

A merchant who wants to accept crypto today picks between two bad shapes: a custodial processor that converts instantly — the merchant never touches the asset and pays a spread for the privilege — or a raw wallet address taped to the register, with no receipts, no refunds, no reporting, and no one to call. CryptoPos is the missing middle: a point of sale where acceptance, settlement policy and rewards are merchant decisions, made once, visible always.

Why it’s hard

Three costs hide behind the QR code. Settlement volatility — between the sale and the sweep, the asset moves; a coffee margin does not survive a 3% move in BTC. Refund semantics — when the price has moved, do you return the tokens or the dollars? Someone loses either way, and nobody has decided who in advance. No dispute rail — every contested charge is a customer-service improvisation. None of these are technology problems. All of them are priced into why merchants say no.

The insight

Merchants change behaviour when the economics pay for it — and card rails have run this exact play for years. Enriched-data programs offer merchants interchange relief, priced in basis points, in exchange for sending richer transaction data. Behaviour followed price. CryptoPos applies the same logic to acceptance: make every cost visible in basis points at configuration time, and let the rewards loop — funded consciously, not accidentally — be the incentive that closes the gap.

What was built

Two surfaces, one economic loop.

  1. USDC is the default asset, volatile assets are opt-in. Volatility is the merchant’s enemy, not their marketing plan — the default protects the margin before the merchant knows to ask.
  2. Settlement policy is set at configuration, not at month-end. The auto-convert threshold is the same decision Parity later expanded into a full treasury surface — this is where I first hit it.
  3. The refund unit is pinned at sale time — fiat value, recorded on the receipt. When the price moves later, the answer to “refund in what?” already exists. Deciding early is the whole feature.
  4. Rewards show their cost in basis points at config time. On card rails, interchange quietly funds rewards. Here the merchant funds them — so the configurator prices each redemption against a margin floor instead of letting generosity become a surprise.
  1. Rewards display in dollars, not tokens. A reward you can’t price isn’t a reward; it’s a lottery ticket. Dollar display is the practice-first belief applied to incentives.
  2. Every payment carries line-item data. The enriched-data parallel made literal: structured receipt data travels with the transaction, and the merchant’s acceptance pricing improves because of it. Data for basis points — the oldest trade on card rails, ported to the new one.

Success criteria

Signups are theatre. Week thirteen is the truth.

The obvious metrics — merchants signed, wallets downloaded, gross crypto volume — all inflate with incentives and say nothing about whether acceptance survived contact with a real quarter of trading. One whale transaction distorts volume for a month. The north star is boring on purpose: merchants still accepting weekly, ninety days in, without a rep on the phone.

What I got wrong · what’s next

What I got wrong

  • I led with asset breadth. Four assets at launch read as ambition; in practice BTC, ETH and SOL acceptance complicated refunds, accounting and the pitch itself. Stablecoin-first with volatile assets as an opt-in flourish is the honest architecture — the prototype’s default hides what its asset list confesses.
  • The first rewards design ignored who pays. I copied card-style “2% back” without copying the thing that funds it. On card rails that’s interchange; here it’s the merchant’s margin. The configurator’s margin floor exists because version one let a demo merchant configure themselves into losing money on every sale.
  • Refunds were an afterthought until they were the whole problem. “Send the tokens back” fails the moment price moves. Pinning refund value at sale was retrofitted, and it decides who loses on the move — a loss-allocation question I should have recognised on sight, given where I work.

What I’d do next: run the program lifecycle on it properly — discovery with a merchant segment where card economics genuinely pinch (small-ticket, high-frequency), a POC gated on the day-90 north star, and a scale/no-scale case built on the settlement-mix data. That exercise is written up as its own page: how I run programs.
