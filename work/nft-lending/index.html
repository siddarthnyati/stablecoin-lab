NFT lending

Lend against an asset with no reliable price and the interface is the least of your problems. The hard parts are oracle design and liquidity — the same problem card rails call thin-file underwriting: deciding, in real time, on an asset you can’t value.

Premise

An NFT holder is asset-rich and liquidity-poor — the crypto version of the borrower whose portfolio doesn’t count. Letting them borrow against the asset unlocks capital without a sale. Every part of that sentence is easy except one word: against. Against what value? Marked by whom? Sold to whom, when it goes wrong?

Why it’s hard

Three compounding problems. No reliable price — NFTs trade sparsely; the “floor price” is one thin order away from fiction, and it’s manipulable by anyone motivated. Correlated liquidity — the bids that would absorb a liquidation evaporate precisely when liquidations cluster, because the same downturn causes both. Adverse selection — the borrowers keenest to pledge an NFT at a generous valuation are the ones who know it’s generous.

The insight

When you can’t price the collateral, price the exit. The lending decision isn’t “what is this worth?” — unanswerable — but “what will it fetch, how fast, in a bad week?” That reframing moves the product’s centre of gravity from valuation to liquidation: conservative loan-to-value set by realised sales rather than floors, and a liquidation path designed before the first loan, not after the first default. Thin-file underwriting reaches the same conclusion on card rails: with no history to price, you price the downside and cap the exposure.

What was built

A lending book that admits what it doesn’t know.

  1. The oracle marks from realised sales, not listings. A trimmed, time-weighted median of actual sales resists the two cheapest attacks — wash-traded spikes and spoofed floors — at the cost of moving slowly, which for a lender is a feature.
  2. LTV is set per collection by exit math. Sparse, volatile sale histories produce low ceilings automatically. No committee decides a collection is “blue chip”; the spread between marks and realised sales does.
  3. The liquidation path exists before the loan. Committed backstop bids and a descending auction with a published clock — so “time-to-liquidate” is a designed number, not a discovery made during a crisis.
  4. Bad debt is a first-class metric on the book screen — not buried in a risk tab. A lending product that hides its bad-debt rate is marketing.

Success criteria

Volume grows fastest when underwriting is worst.

Loan volume — the obvious metric — is actively perverse here: the easiest way to grow it is to over-mark collateral, and adverse selection guarantees the growth arrives. The metrics that matter all live on the exit side.

What I got wrong · what’s next

What I got wrong

  • I started with the gallery. The first prototype was a beautiful grid of collections with a borrow button — UI for a product whose actual product is an oracle. The screens above exist because the honest version of this POC is mostly tables.
  • Version one used the raw floor price. One wash trade or one panicked delisting moved every LTV in the collection. Switching to trimmed realised-sale medians was the single biggest design change — and it came from red-teaming my own oracle, which should have been step one.
  • I modelled liquidity as a constant. The bids I assumed would absorb liquidations are correlated with the crash that triggers them. Time-to-liquidate became a designed, committed number — backstop bids, auction clock — only after I understood the first model was circular.

What I’d do next: backtest the oracle design against public sale histories for a handful of collections and publish the mark-vs-realised error honestly; then stress the auction design against a simulated cluster of simultaneous liquidations — the scenario the whole product exists for.
