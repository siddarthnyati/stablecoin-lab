Parity

A merchant can accept a stablecoin in thirty seconds. Deciding what to do with the balance afterwards — hold it, convert it, sweep it, refund from it — is the actual product, and nobody builds for that.

The thesis

Whoever moves money answers the three questions card networks spent thirty years on. The same $100, both rails:

answered by the rail partially — cryptography, not identity open question

Why this POC

The other four treat USDC as a payment asset. This one treats it as an instrument with a balance sheet behind it — reserves, custody, mint/burn, settlement.

Why it’s hard

Acceptance demos optimise the easy half. After the QR scan, the merchant holds an instrument they can’t price, backed by reserves they’ve never read, with no refund rail.

The insight

Every card-rail cost buys something — fraud screen, authentication, disputes, guaranteed funds. Stablecoins aren’t cheaper; they’re unbundled. Price the buy-back.

2 / 6 · Reserve & attestation

What the balance actually is.

A merchant holding $50,000 of a stablecoin is an unsecured creditor of its issuer. Most don’t know that. This surface reads the fine print for them.

Reserve mix (demo): 80% short-dated T-bills · 12% overnight reverse repo · 8% cash at banks — the slice that failed in March 2023-style bank stress.

What “fully reserved” guarantees

That at the attestation instant, reported reserves ≥ tokens outstanding. It is an accountant’s photograph, not a film.

What it doesn’t guarantee
  • An attestation is not an audit — no opinion on controls.
  • Nothing between attestation dates.
  • Redemption at par, at speed, in stress — gates and queues are legal.
  • The banked-cash slice carries ordinary bank-failure risk.
Why a merchant should care

Holding the coin is a treasury decision with credit exposure — which is why the settlement console’s “hold” path is never actually free. Card rails never ask a merchant to underwrite their acquirer.

3 / 6 · Redemption path

Mint and burn, mechanically.

“Convert to fiat” is one button and five parties. Step through what actually happens — and where the time goes.

Burn walk-through Illustrative timing
Supply outstanding: 1,000,000 USDC (demo)
  1. Merchant — sends 50,000 USDC to the issuer’s redemption address. On-chain leg: seconds to minutes to finality.
  2. Chain — transfer finalises; the tokens now sit with the issuer, not the merchant.
  3. Issuer — burns the 50,000 tokens. Supply outstanding drops; the 1:1 claim is extinguished.
  4. Issuer → reserve bank — instructs release of $50,000 from reserves.
  5. Banking rail — wire or ACH carries fiat to the merchant’s account. This is where the clock lives: cut-off times, business days, compliance checks.
  6. Merchant bank — funds land. End-to-end: same-day to T+1 in the demo — roughly card settlement speed, because the last leg is the same legacy rail.

The uncomfortable symmetry: the chain leg is nearly instant, but redemption ends on the banking rails stablecoins were meant to route around. Against card T+1 net settlement, the advantage is control and finality — not raw speed — until the fiat leg modernises too.

5 / 6 · Refund & dispute — the missing rail

Chargebacks are the feature crypto forgot to copy.

On card rails, a cardholder who gets a brick instead of a laptop has a process: the issuer files, a rulebook allocates liability, and if the transaction was 3DS-authenticated the fraud loss has already shifted from merchant to issuer by design. That machinery is why strangers hand card numbers to strangers. On a stablecoin rail a “refund” is just a brand-new payment the merchant may or may not choose to send. There is no rail — so this surface proposes three, and is explicit about who eats the loss in each.

Uses the settlement console’s monthly volume. Demo arithmetic — the point is that consumer protection has a price, and on card rails that price is already inside the interchange line a merchant pays without itemising it.

The design pattern worth porting isn’t the chargeback form — it’s liability shift as an incentive. EMV 3DS moved fraud loss to whoever performed authentication, and authentication rates followed. A stablecoin dispute rail should do the same: whoever attests — to delivery, to identity, to escrow release — carries the loss when the attestation is wrong. Loss allocation is the product; everything else is plumbing.

A “stable” balance still moved in real markets — depegs have happened to major coins. The rule is boring and correct: hold what you’d be comfortable holding of any single counterparty’s paper, sweep the rest, and write the threshold down before the volatile day, not during it.

Success criteria

The obvious metric is wrong.

The obvious metric is signup count or gross volume — both inflate with incentives and prove nothing. A merchant who accepts a stablecoin once has run an experiment. One who accepts twice, unprompted, has adopted a rail.

What I got wrong · what’s next

What I got wrong

  • I benchmarked against headline interchange. The first cost model compared USDC paths to the card rate and declared victory. Wrong benchmark: interchange buys fraud screening, authentication, dispute handling and a funds guarantee. The honest comparison prices the unbundled services back in — the current console does, and the gap shrank accordingly.
  • I treated “hold” as the cheap path. Zero conversion cost read as zero cost. Holding is a credit position against the issuer plus depeg exposure — surface 6 exists because surface 1 was quietly lying.
  • I assumed the speed story was the chain’s to win. Redemption ends on a wire or ACH leg, so end-to-end time to fiat lands near card settlement. The durable advantages are finality and programmability, not speed — a weaker pitch and a truer one.

What I’d do next: pressure-test the dispute mechanisms with real merchants and a real issuer’s redemption terms; model small-ticket economics separately (the 2¢ flat fee flips the comparison at low tickets); and take the “services unbundled” framing to an issuer or processor as a product thesis — sell protection on the new rail, priced in basis points, the way the old rail always has.
